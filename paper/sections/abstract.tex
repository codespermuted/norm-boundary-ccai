Instance normalization (RevIN and its successors) is widely credited as the decisive component behind modern multi-step forecasters, yet all of its variants estimate future statistics endogenously, from past observations alone. We characterize the boundary of this design choice. In a linear-Gaussian model we derive a closed-form crossover among three estimators---global z-scoring (\rawm), instance normalization (\inm), and covariate-based conditional normalization (\cnm)---showing that the benefit of \inm{} decreases monotonically in the exogenous predictability of the series level and reverses past a critical value $\lamstar$ that shrinks with the forecast horizon. The crossover is predictable \emph{before training} by a Level Predictability Score ($\lps$), the out-of-sample $R^2$ of window-mean levels regressed on covariates, yielding the pre-registered decision rule: use conditional normalization when $\lps \geq \tau$ with $\tau = 0.3$. On the pre-registered main grid of 1{,}794 runs---eight datasets (four exogenously driven energy series, four standard benchmarks) and linear, boosted-tree, and deep backbones---all eight sign predictions hit, and on every exogenous (dataset, horizon) cell the Model Confidence Set retains conditional normalization alone, while instance-normalization variants prevail on the standard benchmarks. When every arm is given the same covariates, instance normalization still underperforms even global z-scoring in mean MSE on every backbone, indicating a function-class constraint rather than an information advantage. The pattern replicates across all 4{,}202 runs of the study, including state-of-the-art covariate architectures. The rule is robust for $\tau \in [0.30, 0.70]$. We release the diagnostic as a practical pre-training tool for choosing a normalization strategy.
