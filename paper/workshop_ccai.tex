\title{When Instance Normalization Hurts Energy Forecasting:\\
A Pre-Training Test for the Normalization Layer}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
On power grids, forecast errors for wind, solar, and load are absorbed by
fossil-fuelled spinning reserves and by curtailment. A forecaster's accuracy at
ramps and regime transitions therefore carries a direct emissions footprint. Yet
modern forecasters handle the series \emph{level} with
instance normalization (RevIN and its successors), which estimates that level
\emph{endogenously}, from the target's own past. On wind, solar, and
load---whose level is set by weather observable in advance through archived
numerical forecasts---this discards recoverable signal, and no downstream capacity
restores it. We give a pre-training test---the Level Predictability Score
(\lps)---that flags this failure from the covariates alone, before any model is
trained. In a pre-registered study whose per-dataset predictions were committed to
version control before any run, the test's sign is correct on all eight datasets.
Under information parity---every model handed the same covariates---instance
normalization is worse in mean MSE than plain global scaling on all five backbones,
confirming a covariate-case conjecture recent work had left open; and on a regional
wind fleet, conditioning the level on covariates instead cuts capacity-normalized
error by $8.4\%$. When \lps{} clears its threshold the remedy is a one-line change
to the normalization layer, so the diagnostic is a near-zero-cost screen for the
level-handling component of energy-forecasting pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and contributions}

Renewable generation and electricity demand are forecasting problems in which the
\emph{level} of the series is set by observable, physically grounded drivers.
Wind power is installed capacity times a weather-regime factor; photovoltaic
output follows a clear-sky geometry modulated by cloud cover; load responds to
temperature through a degree-day relationship. In each case the level to be
predicted is largely exogenous and observable in advance through archived
numerical weather prediction (NWP) available at the forecast origin
\citep{hong2016gefcom}. Conditioning forecasts on these drivers is standard
practice, from the GEFCom competitions to operational wind and load models
\citep{hong2016gefcom,pinson2013wind}.

Modern deep forecasters, however, lead with instance normalization (RevIN;
\citealp{kim2022revin}), which removes each input window's mean and variance
before the backbone and restores them at the output. Its successors refine
\emph{which endogenous statistics} to use---slice-level statistics
\citep{liu2023san}, frequency components \citep{ye2024fan}, learned
distribution coefficients \citep{fan2023dishts}---but all of them estimate the
statistics they restore from the target's own recent past. On an exogenously
driven series the subtracted window mean is a stale summary precisely when a
weather ramp or heat wave is about to move the level, and the staleness compounds
with the forecast horizon. For climate applications this is the common case, not
a corner case: ramps and regime transitions are exactly the events that drive
reserve scheduling, curtailment, and renewable integration.

This default is now baked into covariate forecasters without being tested:
architectures built to ingest exogenous variables apply instance normalization to
the target channel and assume it helps, running no ablation against turning it off
\citep{wang2024timexer,crosslinear2025}. Recent work questions the assumption in
principle---instance normalization is a strong inductive bias that, by removing
scale and offset, ``discards potentially predictive context''---but only in the
univariate, covariate-free case, and it explicitly leaves ours open: ``a more
general setting would include \ldots exogenous covariates \ldots although we
anticipate similar conclusions, we do not consider it here'' \citep{role2026revin}.
We test that conjecture directly, under information parity, and find it holds on
exogenously driven energy series.

We ask when this default helps and when it hurts, and reduce the answer to a
quantity computable \emph{before} any model is trained. Our contributions are
(i)~an information-fair demonstration---the first controlled test of the covariate
case \citet{role2026revin} left as a conjecture---that the cost is a property of the
normalization \emph{layer}, not of covariate access (\S\ref{sec:results}); (ii)~a
pre-training diagnostic, the Level Predictability Score, with an explicit
decision rule (\S\ref{sec:score}); and (iii)~a pre-registered study, with
per-dataset predictions committed to version control before any run, in which the
rule is correct on all eight datasets and the pattern replicates across
$4{,}202$ runs including state-of-the-art covariate architectures
(\S\ref{sec:results}). We do \emph{not} propose a new normalizer: the
covariate-conditional normalization we use to expose the boundary is itself
beaten by cheaper covariate-conditioned baselines, which under our thesis is
consistent evidence, not a weakness---the problem is the layer, not any one
replacement for it.

\section{A boundary and a pre-training score}\label{sec:score}

Consider three ways to handle the level: global $z$-scoring (\rawm), instance
normalization (\inm), and covariate-based conditional normalization (\cnm), which
estimates the target-time level from a first-stage regression on covariates.
Writing any \inm{}-equipped backbone as
$\hat y = \bar y + s\, f\!\left((x-\bar y\mathbf 1)/s,\, g\right)$, its dependence on
the window mean $\bar y$ is additive with unit slope: before seeing the data the
layer commits to a level response of slope one with no level--covariate interaction.
Whenever the window mean is a stale estimate of the target-time level, that is an
irreducible cost no downstream flexibility removes. A linear--Gaussian analysis makes
the trade quantitative: \inm{} pays the frozen statistic's within-horizon drift
$s_x^2(h)+h\sigma_u^2$, while \cnm{} pays the covariate-unexplained level variance
$(1-\lambda)V$, where $\lambda\in[0,1]$ is the exogenous predictability of the level.
So \cnm{} wins iff $\lambda>\lambda^\star$, and the crossover $\lambda^\star$ falls
with the horizon $h$---multi-step forecasting is where the endogenous default is most
exposed (full derivation in the extended version).

The exogenous predictability is unobservable before training, so we estimate it
out of sample. Partitioning the training series into non-overlapping length-$w$
windows with means $(\bar y_i, \bar x_i)$ and regressing the former on the latter
under expanding chronological cross-validation gives the \textbf{Level
Predictability Score},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lps}
\lps \;=\; 1 \;-\;
\frac{\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_k}\bigl(\bar y_i-\hat g_k(\bar x_i)\bigr)^2}
     {\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_k}\bigl(\bar y_i-\bar y^{\,\mathrm{tr}}_k\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
the out-of-sample $R^2$ of window-mean levels on covariates ($\hat g_k$: LightGBM,
$w{=}96$, fit only on windows preceding block $\mathcal{T}_k$). The decision rule
is one line, stated in the direction the evidence supports:
\[
\lps \ge \tau \;\Rightarrow\; \text{do not set the level endogenously}; \qquad
\text{otherwise instance normalization}; \qquad \tau = 0.3 .
\]
Above the threshold the level should be conditioned on covariates by whatever means
the pipeline supports (\S\ref{sec:results}); the threshold is anchored to the
stylized crossover ($\approx 0.27$--$0.28$ at $h{=}24$), conservative toward
instance normalization. We validate this rule as a \emph{regime classifier}: our
datasets sit at the two ends of the \lps{} range, so it separates the regimes but
does not yet resolve a precise cut, and a dataset of intermediate predictability is
the key confirmatory target.

\paragraph{Setup.} The pre-registered grid crosses five normalization arms
\{\rawm, RevIN, SAN, FAN, \cnm\} \citep{kim2022revin,liu2023san,ye2024fan} with
four backbones \{RLinear, PatchTST, SegRNN, LightGBM\}
\citep{li2023rlinear,nie2023patchtst,lin2023segrnn,ke2017lightgbm}, three horizons,
and five seeds, on eight datasets in two groups: four exogenously driven energy
series (Jeju Wind, curated for this study with lead-matched archived KMA forecasts;
GEFCom-Wind/Load/Solar, \citealp{hong2016gefcom}) and four standard LTSF
benchmarks. The three GEFCom sets share a vintage but cover distinct physical
processes; Jeju Wind is independently curated. All NWP covariates are archived
operational forecasts, never reanalysis. Splits are chronological and out-of-time;
the lookback is tuned on the RevIN arm and frozen across arms, so arms differ only
in normalization.

The dissociation is symmetric. \cnm{} is catastrophic on ETTh2 ($6.60$) for the
mirror reason: there the only covariates are calendar features with no exogenous
level signal, so the first stage fits noise and the restore step injects it---the
failure the diagnostic pre-empts, since ETTh2's $\lps$ ($-0.205$) sits well below
$\tau$, in the \emph{use instance normalization} regime. This is why the rule is
one-sided.

\paragraph{It is the layer, not the information.} A natural objection is that \cnm{}
wins only because it \emph{sees} the covariates. An information-fair block hands
\emph{every} arm the identical past and future covariates, across five backbones.
There the isolated cost of the layer, $\mse(\text{RevIN}{+}\text{cov}) -
\mse(\rawm{+}\text{cov})$, is positive on all five and significant (95\% CI
excluding zero) on four, null on PatchTST-Cov: instance normalization is worse than
plain scaling even given the same covariates, because the restore step overwrites a
level they carry. The effect is small ($+0.004$ to $+0.024$ MSE)---the honest size
of the layer's cost once access is equalized, one to two orders below the head-line
gap of Table~\ref{tab:mini}, which measures access.

On the published covariate architectures (TimeXer, iTransformer) the toggle is
instead \emph{mixed}---their dedicated covariate pathways partly mask the layer,
which is why a controlled generic-backbone ablation is the right instrument for
isolating it.

\paragraph{A strategy-level dissociation.} The same contrast holds across
\emph{strategies}, not one method: against seasonal-naive and climatology anchors,
three covariate-conditioned level models---a first-stage regression, a classical
dynamic regression, and \cnm---beat the best instance-normalized arm on $11$ of
$12$ exogenous comparisons and $0$ of $12$ standard ones ($10/12$ and $0/12$ under
MASE), the two exceptions being the linear dynamic regression on GEFCom-Load.

\paragraph{The pre-training rule.} The official \lps{} value and the predicted
sign of the RevIN$-$\cnm{} gap for each dataset were committed to version control
(git commit \texttt{cab17c1}) before any grid run; all eight signs were confirmed,
unchanged under MSE, MAE, and MASE (Fig.~\ref{fig:lpsgap}), and in all eleven
exogenous cells the Model Confidence Set retains \cnm{} alone. The bimodality has an upside for the rule: the eight-dataset classification is
unchanged for any $\tau\in[0.30,0.70]$ (it need only fall in the empty gap between
the clusters), and $\tau$ is anchored to the stylized crossover, so the decision
rests on an analytic argument, not fitted spacing. Within the exogenous regime,
however, \lps{} does not order the size of the gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:lpsgap}).

\section{Implications for energy operations, and limitations}

The boundary is operational: wind, solar, and load move at the ramps and regime
transitions that drive reserve scheduling and curtailment. Because \lps{} needs only
covariates and a first-stage regression already in most pipelines, it is a
near-zero-cost screen---seconds on a CPU, no forecasting model---run before
GPU-hours are spent. On Jeju Wind, conditioning the level on covariates cuts
capacity-normalized MAE from $0.110$ (RevIN) to $0.101$ (\cnm)---$8.4\%$ in the units
an operator sizes reserves in. The chain from forecast accuracy to reserves to
emissions is established: improved forecasting cuts operating-reserve requirements
and system emissions \citep{sun2022mlreserve}, and displaced fossil generation
carries of order $0.5$\,tCO$_2$/MWh \citep{kaffine2013emissions}. As an
\emph{illustrative} scale check---a $300$\,MW fleet with reserve taken proportional
to error---the $8.4\%$ reduction is of order a few thousand tCO$_2$/yr, modest per
installation but scalable across fleets. This is not a dispatch analysis: it ignores
grid topology, unit commitment, and market structure, which the cited studies model
and we do not. We report the forecast accuracy and defer the grid-specific figure to
those tools.

\paragraph{What to use above the threshold.} Stop setting the level from the
lookback window; the covariate-conditioned replacements are close on the exogenous
group ($0.29/0.16/0.14/0.06$ MSE for a first-stage regression, $0.29/0.16/0.11/0.06$
for \cnm), so choose by pipeline fit. The cheapest deep-stack drop-in is conditional
normalization---feed the backbone the first-stage level in place of the window mean,
leaving it untouched---and a classical stack has its analogue in regression with
ARIMA errors. The step is the switch away from endogenous statistics, not any one
method.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our results are \emph{point} forecasts: the same boundary
appears in pinball loss, but conditional normalization \emph{under-covers} its
intervals ($0.50$--$0.66$ at nominal $0.80$)---a calibration artifact of the
first-stage residuals, orthogonal to level handling and fixable by post-hoc
recalibration. Evaluation uses a single origin; rolling-origin re-evaluation and
intermediate-\lps{} datasets are the confirmatory targets.
