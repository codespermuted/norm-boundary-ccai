\title{When Instance Normalization Hurts Energy Forecasting:\\
A Pre-Training Test for the Normalization Layer}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
On power grids, forecast errors for wind, solar, and load are absorbed by
fossil-fuelled reserves and curtailment; accuracy at ramps carries a direct
emissions footprint. Yet modern forecasters hand
the series \emph{level} to instance normalization (RevIN and successors), which
estimates it \emph{endogenously} from the target's own past. On wind, solar, and
load---whose level is set by weather observable in advance through archived
numerical forecasts---this discards recoverable signal, and no \emph{generic}
downstream capacity restores it. A pre-training test, the Level Predictability
Score (\lps), flags this failure from covariates alone.
In a pre-registered study, with per-dataset predictions committed to
version control before any run, the sign is correct on all eight datasets, and
consistent across the ten zones of a wind-fleet disaggregation. Under information
parity, where every model is handed the same covariates, instance normalization
costs accuracy over plain global scaling on four of five backbones (95\% CIs
excluding zero; the fifth is null), supporting a covariate-case conjecture
recent work left open. Conditioning the level on covariates instead cuts
day-ahead capacity-normalized error on a regional wind fleet by $5\%$. Above
the threshold the remedy is a one-line change to the normalization layer, making
\lps{} a near-zero-cost screen for energy-forecasting pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and contributions}

Renewable generation and electricity demand are forecasting problems in which the
\emph{level} of the series is set by observable, physically grounded drivers.
Wind power follows the weather regime, photovoltaic output follows cloud
cover, and load follows temperature. In each case the level to be
predicted is largely exogenous and, in operations, observable in advance through
numerical weather prediction (NWP). Conditioning forecasts on these drivers is
standard practice, from the GEFCom competitions---which shipped target-period
ECMWF forecasts for wind and solar---to operational wind and load models
\citep{hong2016gefcom,pinson2013wind}.

Modern deep forecasters, however, lead with instance normalization (RevIN;
\citealp{kim2022revin}): each input window's mean and variance are removed
before the backbone and restored at the output. Successors refine \emph{which
endogenous statistics} to use \citep{liu2023san,ye2024fan,fan2023dishts}, but
all of them estimate what they restore from the target's own recent past. On an
exogenously driven series, the subtracted window mean is stale exactly when a
weather ramp or heat wave is about to move the level, and the staleness
compounds with horizon. For climate applications this is the common case: ramps and regime transitions
drive reserve scheduling and curtailment.

This default is now baked into covariate forecasters without being tested.
CrossLinear attaches a RevIN module to the target channel and ablates only its
embedding, never the normalization \citep{crosslinear2025}. TimeXer's paper
never discusses input normalization, yet its official implementation normalizes
the endogenous channel by default \citep{wang2024timexer}. The community, in
short, ships covariate architectures whose level handling silently fights the
covariates. Recent work questions the endogenous default in principle---instance
normalization ``discards potentially predictive context''---but only in the
univariate case, and it explicitly leaves ours open: ``a more general setting
would include \ldots exogenous covariates \ldots although we anticipate similar
conclusions, we do not consider it here'' \citep{role2026revin}. Pre-training normalization
selectors exist, but they read
endogenous change-point statistics \citep{noise2025signal}; module-level
benchmarking conditions instance-normalization use on intrinsic dataset
properties \citep{timerecipe2025}. Neither admits covariate-conditioned level
handling into the choice set, and prior conditional normalization
\citep{gamakumara2023conditional} does not ask the selection question.

We ask when this default helps and when it hurts, and reduce the answer to a
quantity computable before training. The claim is not that covariates help
(standard practice), but that the shipped default quietly works against them,
and that the failure is predictable before training. Our contributions are
(i)~an information-fair demonstration---the first controlled test of the covariate
case \citet{role2026revin} left as a conjecture---that the cost is a property of the
normalization \emph{layer}, not of covariate access (\S\ref{sec:results}); (ii)~a
pre-training diagnostic, the Level Predictability Score, validated as a
\emph{two-regime classifier} (\S\ref{sec:score}); and (iii)~a pre-registered
study---per-dataset predictions committed to version control before any
run---in which the predicted sign is correct on all eight datasets and
consistent across the ten zones of a graded follow-up, replicating across
$4{,}202$ runs including
state-of-the-art covariate architectures (\S\ref{sec:results}). We do \emph{not}
propose a new normalizer. The conditional normalization we use to expose the
boundary is itself beaten by cheaper covariate-conditioned baselines; under our
thesis that is consistent evidence, because the problem is the layer, not any
one replacement.

\section{A boundary and a pre-training score}\label{sec:score}

We compare three ways of handling the level: global $z$-scoring (\rawm), instance
normalization (\inm), and covariate-conditional normalization (\cnm), which takes
the target-time level from a first-stage regression on covariates.
Writing any \inm{}-equipped backbone as
$\hat y = \bar y + s\, f\!\left((x-\bar y\mathbf 1)/s,\, g\right)$, its dependence on
the window mean $\bar y$ is additive with unit slope: the layer commits, before
seeing data, to a slope-one level response with no level--covariate
interaction---an irreducible cost whenever the window mean is a stale estimate of
the target-time level. A linear--Gaussian analysis makes the trade quantitative:
\inm{} pays the frozen statistic's within-horizon drift $s_x^2(h)+h\sigma_u^2$,
\cnm{} pays the covariate-unexplained level variance $(1-\lambda)V$, with
$\lambda\in[0,1]$ the exogenous predictability of the level; \cnm{} wins iff
$\lambda>\lambda^\star$, and $\lambda^\star$ falls with the horizon---multi-step
forecasting is where the endogenous default is most exposed (derivation and the numerical anchor: Appendix~\ref{app:theory}).

$\lambda$ is unobservable before training, so we estimate it out of sample:
partition the training series into non-overlapping length-$w$ windows with means
$(\bar y_i, \bar x_i)$ and regress the former on the latter under expanding
chronological cross-validation, giving the \textbf{Level Predictability Score},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lps}
\lps \;=\; 1 \;-\;
\frac{\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_k}\bigl(\bar y_i-\hat g_k(\bar x_i)\bigr)^2}
     {\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_k}\bigl(\bar y_i-\bar y^{\,\mathrm{tr}}_k\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
the out-of-sample $R^2$ of window-mean levels on covariates ($\hat g_k$: LightGBM,
$w{=}96$, fit only on windows preceding block $\mathcal{T}_k$). The decision rule is one line:
$\lps \ge \tau \Rightarrow$ \emph{do not set the level endogenously}; otherwise
instance normalization ($\tau = 0.3$).
The threshold is anchored to the stylized crossover ($\approx0.28$ at $h{=}24$;
$0.26$--$0.28$ across horizons), conservative toward instance normalization;
above it, condition the level on covariates by whatever means the pipeline
supports (\S\ref{sec:results}). We validate the rule as a \emph{two-regime
classifier}: our datasets populate both sides of the boundary but leave the
$0.28$--$0.58$ interior unobserved, so the evidence supports the regime call,
not a calibrated cut.

\paragraph{Setup.} The pre-registered grid crosses five normalization arms
\{\rawm, RevIN, SAN, FAN, \cnm\} \citep{kim2022revin,liu2023san,ye2024fan} with
four backbones \{RLinear, PatchTST, SegRNN, LightGBM\}
\citep{li2023rlinear,nie2023patchtst,lin2023segrnn,ke2017lightgbm}, the horizons
of Table~\ref{tab:mini}, and five seeds, on eight datasets in two groups: four
exogenously driven energy series---Jeju Wind (self-curated from archived KMA
operational forecasts; the development dataset) and GEFCom-Wind/Load/Solar
\citep{hong2016gefcom} (third-party and confirmation-style, as are the four
standard LTSF benchmarks). NWP covariates
for the wind and solar sets are archived operational forecasts, never reanalysis.
Two exceptions are disclosed in Limitations: GEFCom-Load's temperature is
realized rather than forecast, and Jeju Wind's $h{=}48$ cells inherit a
day-ahead forecast band. Splits are
chronological and out-of-time; lookback is tuned on the RevIN arm and frozen
across arms---arms differ only in normalization (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}).

The dissociation is symmetric. \cnm{} is catastrophic on ETTh2 ($6.60$) because
its only covariates are calendar features: the first stage fits noise and the
restore step injects it. The diagnostic pre-empts exactly this failure
($\lps=-0.205$, well below $\tau$); this is why the rule is one-sided.

\paragraph{It is the layer, not the information.} A natural objection is that
\cnm{} wins only because it sees the covariates. An information-fair block
therefore hands every arm identical past and future covariates, across five
covariate-capable backbones (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). The isolated cost of the
layer, $\mse(\text{RevIN}{+}\text{cov}) - \mse(\rawm{+}\text{cov})$, is positive
in the mean on all five backbones and significant (95\% CI excluding zero) on
four; on PatchTST-Cov it is null. Even handed the same covariates, instance
normalization is worse than plain scaling, because the restore step overwrites a
level the covariates carry. The effect is small, $+0.004$ to $+0.024$ MSE. That
is the honest layer-only cost: one to two orders below Table~\ref{tab:mini}'s
headline gaps, which measure access. The dissociation itself replicates on
TimeXer and iTransformer, where \cnm{} is best on all four exogenous datasets
(mean gap $+0.41$) \citep{wang2024timexer,liu2024itransformer}; the isolated
RevIN-vs-\rawm{} toggle there is \emph{mixed}, because dedicated covariate
pathways partly mask the layer---hence the controlled ablation above.

\paragraph{A strategy-level dissociation.} The same contrast holds across
\emph{strategies}, not one method: against seasonal-naive and climatology anchors,
three covariate-conditioned level models---a first-stage regression, a ridge
dynamic regression, and \cnm---beat the best instance-normalized arm on $11/12$
exogenous comparisons and $0/12$ standard ones ($10/12$ and $0/12$ under MASE);
every exception is the ridge dynamic regression (GEFCom-Load under MSE, plus
GEFCom-Solar under MASE).

\paragraph{The pre-training rule.} The official \lps{} and predicted gap sign
for each dataset were committed to version control four minutes before the first
grid run (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). All eight signs were confirmed, unchanged
under MSE, MAE, and MASE (Fig.~\ref{fig:lpsgap}); the datasets cluster into two
correlated regimes, so we read $8/8$ as per-dataset evidence, not eight
independent successes. All eleven exogenous (dataset,\,$h$) Model Confidence Sets retain
\cnm{} alone (caveats: Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). The classification is
unchanged over $\tau\in[0.30,0.70]$---insensitivity that reflects the empty
interior (\S\ref{sec:score}), not fine calibration.

\paragraph{A graded test within one family.} We therefore pre-registered a
harder test: GEFCom-Wind disaggregates into ten zones of genuinely different NWP
skill (\lps{} $0.575$--$0.758$, identical covariate structure), with per-zone
\lps{} and predictions committed before any zone run. The sign rule
holds in all ten zones (a consistency check: every zone sits above $\tau$), and
the RevIN$-$\cnm{} gap widens with horizon
($0.53 \to 0.79 \to 0.90$ at $h{=}24/96/336$), as the theory predicts.
The pre-registered magnitude prediction is \emph{not} confirmed: Spearman
$+0.47$ at $n{=}10$ ($p{=}0.17$) is underpowered and inconclusive, so \lps{}
stands as a validated regime classifier, not a calibrated dial
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lpsgap}; a second unconfirmed secondary:
Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). Reporting the failed endpoint is the point of
pre-registration: it closes a dial over-reading a post-hoc analysis could have
kept.

\section{Implications for energy operations, and limitations}

\lps{} needs only covariates and a first-stage regression already in most
pipelines: about half a CPU-second per series, against the ${\approx}180$
GPU-hours our own grid logged (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). On Jeju Wind at the day-ahead horizon,
conditioning the level on covariates cuts capacity-normalized MAE from $0.105$
to $0.100$ (RevIN vs \cnm{} on the information-parity linear mixer).
Capacity-normalized error is the unit reserve procurement scales with: this
$5.0\%$ is roughly $1.6$\,MW of standing reserve on a $300$\,MW fleet. The chain to emissions is established: sharper
characterization of forecast uncertainty cuts reserves and emissions in
production simulation \citep{sun2022mlreserve}, and
displaced fossil generation carries $\approx0.5$\,tCO$_2$/MWh ($0.49$--$0.83$
across U.S.\ regions and hours) \citep{kaffine2013emissions,silerevans2012marginal}.
At full displacement the $5\%$ cut is of order a few thousand tCO$_2$/yr, less
under part-load holding penalties. The scale check is illustrative: one fleet,
one horizon, no grid topology, unit commitment, or market structure, and it
reads from the point forecast, so it becomes operational only once intervals are
calibrated (Limitations). Above the
threshold, the replacements perform within hundredths of one another
(Appendix~\ref{app:setup}), so choose by pipeline fit: the cheapest deep-stack drop-in feeds the backbone the first-stage
level in place of the window mean; regression with ARIMA errors is the classical
analogue (not benchmarked here).

\paragraph{Limitations.} Two provenance caveats bound the exogenous numbers:
GEFCom-Load's temperature is \emph{realized}, not forecast (attainable, not
operational, skill), and Jeju Wind's $h{=}48$ cells consume a band valid to
$h\le24$ (pooled figures optimistic); the day-ahead figure above avoids both. The boundary
transfers to pinball loss (\cnm{} best on all four exogenous sets), but \cnm{}
\emph{under-covers} its intervals ($0.50$--$0.66$ at nominal $0.80$):
first-stage uncertainty is unpropagated; recalibration not yet run.
Evaluation uses one origin; rolling origins and gap-interior datasets come
next (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}).

\section{Setup details, accounting, and availability}\label{app:setup}

\paragraph{Data and splits.} Hourly resolution throughout. Exogenous group: Jeju
Wind (self-curated from archived KMA operational forecasts, lead-matched at
$h{=}24$; development dataset) and GEFCom2014 Wind/Load/Solar
\citep{hong2016gefcom} (competition-provided target-time forecast covariates for
wind and solar; realized station temperature for load). Standard group: ETTh1,
ETTh2, Electricity, Weather under literature splits (ETT 12/4/4 months; 7:1:2
elsewhere); zone series use chronological 60/20/20. Global $z$-scoring is fit on
the train segment only; splits are strictly out-of-time.

\paragraph{Run accounting.} Main grid $1{,}794$ runs: 5 arms $\times$ 4 backbones
$\times$ $h\in\{24,96,336\}$ (Jeju Wind $\{24,48\}$, set by forecast-band
coverage---hence \emph{eleven} exogenous (dataset,\,$h$) cells) $\times$ 5 seeds;
LightGBM$\times$\{SAN, FAN\} is structurally inapplicable (torch statistics
predictors). Information-parity block $1{,}133$ runs over five covariate-capable
backbones: LightGBM-Cov, linear mixer, MLP mixer, SegRNN-Cov, PatchTST-Cov, every
arm given identical past and future covariates. SOTA replication $1{,}275$ runs
(TimeXer, iTransformer; built-in normalization removed, arms injected). Total
$4{,}202$. The graded zone study adds $450$ pre-registered RLinear runs (10 zones
$\times$ 3 arms $\times$ 3 horizons $\times$ 5 seeds); a deferred LightGBM arm is
incomplete and excluded from the primary endpoint, disclosed at pre-registration
analysis time. The 2{,}640-run synthetic grid behind
Appendix~\ref{app:theory} is a separate tally, outside the $4{,}202$.

\paragraph{Compute.} Logged wall-clock for the runs in the frozen main-grid file
(main grid plus SOTA replications) totals ${\approx}180$ hours on a single
consumer GPU; the information-parity block's wall-clock was not logged. The
\lps{} screen itself costs ${\approx}0.5$\,s per univariate series on one CPU
core (measured on GEFCom-Wind; the full eight-dataset panel, including every
channel of the multivariate benchmarks, completes in minutes). This is the
contrast behind \S4's ``near-zero-cost screen'': the diagnostic costs roughly
six orders of magnitude less than the study that validated it.

\paragraph{Inference.} Information-parity confidence intervals are cluster
bootstraps over the eleven seed-averaged (dataset,\,$h$) cells. The Model
Confidence Set is computed at $\alpha=0.10$ per (dataset,\,$h$) on
backbone-averaged squared-error series; survivor sets are stable across bootstrap
seeds. The MCS is a singleton in 20 of 23 cells on this single evaluation path,
so we read it as a compact ranking consistent with the pairwise tests, not an
uncertainty set. Zone-study secondaries: the endogenous RevIN$-$\rawm{} per-zone
prediction is not confirmed (positive in 4 of 10 zones; Spearman $-0.48$,
$p{=}0.16$) --- the layer's small cost, of parity-block magnitude, sits below
zone-level noise. Replacement costs on the exogenous group (test MSE,
jeju/wind/load/solar): first-stage regression $0.29/0.16/0.14/0.06$, \cnm{}
$0.29/0.16/0.11/0.06$. All standard-group loss series are segment-aware (no
windows across curated gaps). Single-origin caveat: promoting the validation
segment to a second chronological origin reproduces all four exogenous signs,
so the headline dissociation is not an artifact of the one test path.

\paragraph{LPS protocol.} Non-overlapping windows of length $w=96$; first stage
LightGBM under expanding chronological cross-validation, fit only on windows
preceding each evaluation block (Eq.~\ref{eq:lps}); $\tau=0.3$ was fixed at
pre-registration together with the per-dataset sign predictions. The main-grid
pre-registration commit precedes the first grid run by four minutes; the zone
study's per-zone \lps{} values and predictions were likewise committed before any
zone run. Both are timestamped in the repository below.

\section{The stylized crossover behind $\tau$}\label{app:theory}

\paragraph{Model (M1, linear--Gaussian).} One forecast instance: the target-time
level is $\ell_T = \Delta + g_T + v_T$, with covariate-explained component
$g_T \sim \mathcal N(0, \lambda V)$, unexplained component
$v_T \sim \mathcal N(0, (1-\lambda)V)$, and a covariate-orthogonal
train$\to$test shift $\Delta \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_\Delta^2)$ common to
window and target. The lookback window sees stale components
$g_w = g_T - \delta_x$ and $v_w = v_T - \delta_u$, where
$\delta_x \sim \mathcal N(0, s_x^2(h))$ is the within-horizon covariate-driven
level change (the ramp) and $\delta_u \sim \mathcal N(0, h\sigma_u^2)$ is
random-walk drift; the window mean adds shape noise
$\bar\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_z^2/w)$. \cnm's first stage observes
$\hat m = g_T + e$ with $e \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{est}}^2)$. All
components are independent.

\paragraph{Risks and crossover.} Direct variance accounting of the
level-restoration error (a shape residual $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ is common to
all arms and drops out of comparisons) gives
\[
\mse_{\inm} = s_x^2(h) + h\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_z^2/w, \qquad
\mse_{\cnm} = (1-\lambda)V + \sigma_\Delta^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{est}}^2 :
\]
\inm{} pays the staleness of its frozen statistic, \cnm{} pays the unexplained
level variance. Setting the two equal and solving for $\lambda$ gives the
crossover of \S\ref{sec:score},
\[
\lambda^\star \;=\; 1 \;-\;
\frac{s_x^2(h) + h\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_z^2/w - \sigma_\Delta^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{est}}^2}{V},
\]
and $\partial_h\bigl[\mse_{\inm} - \mse_{\cnm}\bigr] = \sigma_u^2 + \partial_h
s_x^2 > 0$, so the gap grows and $\lambda^\star$ falls with the horizon.

\paragraph{Numerical anchor.} The anchor is measured on the validation DGP of
the 2{,}640-run synthetic grid, not assumed. The DGP sets
$m_t = \sqrt{\lambda}\,x^\ell_t + \sqrt{1-\lambda}\,u^\ell_t$, where $x^\ell$
is the level of an AR(1) covariate ($a{=}0.9$), $u^\ell$ is a standardized
random walk, and both are rescaled so that their $w{=}96$ window means have
unit variance --- hence $V{=}1$, and $\lambda$ coincides with the level $R^2$
by construction (validated to within $0.08$ in the released tests). The
covariate-driven ramp therefore scales with $\lambda$ and the random-walk
drift with $1-\lambda$, each living in its own component of the level split;
this is why $\lambda^\star$ does not collapse at long horizons --- the drift a
high-$\lambda$ series suffers is small by construction. No separate
covariate-orthogonal shift is injected ($\sigma_\Delta^2 = 0$, a CN-favorable
choice, since \inm{} is immune to such shifts), and $\sigma_{\mathrm{est}}^2$
is realized by an actual fitted first stage rather than set. On this DGP the
measured restoration errors --- $\mathbb E[(m_T-\bar y_w)^2]$ for \inm{}
versus $\mathbb E[(m_T-\hat m_T)^2]$ for \cnm{} --- cross at
$\lambda^\star = 0.278$--$0.283$ for $h{=}24$ (lookbacks 96/336), declining to
$0.259$--$0.271$ at $h{=}336$: the ${\approx}0.28$ anchor of
\S\ref{sec:score}. The closed forms above are pinned against Monte Carlo at
${<}1\%$ relative error in the released test suite.

\paragraph{Two conservatism notes.} First, the restoration-rule crossover is
an \emph{upper bound}: trained linear predictors on the same synthetic grid
cross far lower ($\lambda^\star_{\mathrm{OLS}} \approx 0.007$--$0.029$ under
this parameterization), because backbones implicitly track residual level.
Second, neither number transports to the \lps{} axis: \lps{} is a proxy on a
different scale from $\lambda$ --- it absorbs first-stage estimation error and
window-mean smoothing, so it is not a consistent estimator of $\lambda$, and
crossovers computed on the $\lambda$ scale cannot be read off
Fig.~\ref{fig:lpsgap}. $\tau$ was fixed on the \lps{} scale at
pre-registration, with the M1 bound anchoring its order of magnitude. The two
known gaps err in the same direction: the M1 bound overstates the crossover,
and first-stage estimation error biases \lps{} downward relative to
$\lambda$ --- both toward instance normalization, which is the direction the
one-sided rule requires, since a dataset misclassified below $\tau$ forgoes a
gain rather than risking the ETTh2-style failure.
