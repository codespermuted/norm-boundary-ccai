\section{Problem and contributions}
Renewable generation and electricity demand are forecasting problems in which
the target's \emph{level} is set by observable, physically grounded drivers.
Wind power is installed capacity times a weather-regime factor; photovoltaic
output follows a clear-sky geometry modulated by cloud cover; electricity load
responds to temperature through a degree-day relationship. In each case the
level to be predicted is largely \emph{exogenous} and observable in advance
through archived numerical weather prediction (NWP) forecasts available at the
forecast origin \citep{hong2016gefcom}.

Modern multi-step forecasters lead with instance normalization (RevIN and its
successors; \citealp{kim2022revin}), which removes each input window's mean and
variance before the backbone and restores them at the output. This is an
effective defense against train--test distribution shift, but the statistics it
restores are estimated \emph{endogenously}, from the target's own recent past.
On exogenously driven series it therefore discards level information the
covariates carry: the subtracted window mean is a stale summary precisely when a
weather ramp or heat wave is about to move the level, and the staleness
compounds with the forecast horizon. For climate applications this is the
common case, not a corner case---ramps and regime transitions are exactly the
events that drive reserve scheduling, curtailment, and renewable integration.

We ask when this default helps and when it hurts, and reduce the answer to a
quantity computable \emph{before} any model is trained. Our contributions are
(i)~a closed-form characterization of the boundary at which instance
normalization stops helping, in a linear--Gaussian model
(\S\ref{sec:boundary}); (ii)~a pre-training diagnostic---the Level
Predictability Score (LPS)---with an explicit decision rule
(\S\ref{sec:boundary}); and (iii)~an exogenous-versus-endogenous contrast of
\emph{normalization statistics}, evaluated in a pre-registered study whose
per-dataset sign predictions were committed to version control before any
experiment was run (\S\ref{sec:results}). We do not claim conditional
normalization as a new mechanism; the contribution is the boundary, the
diagnostic, and the pre-registered evidence that places each dataset on one
side of it.
