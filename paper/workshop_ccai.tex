\title{When Instance Normalization Hurts Energy Forecasting:\\
A Pre-Training Diagnostic for Choosing a Normalization Strategy}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Instance normalization (RevIN and its successors) is the default level-handling
component of modern multi-step forecasters, yet it estimates the level it
restores \emph{endogenously}, from the target's own past. On exogenously driven
energy series---wind, solar, and load, whose levels are set by weather
observable in advance through numerical weather forecasts---this discards
recoverable signal. We show in closed form that instance normalization helps
only below a critical exogenous level-predictability $\lamstar$ and hurts above
it, and we reduce the crossover to a pre-training diagnostic: the Level
Predictability Score (LPS), the out-of-sample $R^2$ of window-mean levels
regressed on covariates, with the rule ``use conditional normalization iff
$\lps \ge 0.3$.'' In a pre-registered study whose per-dataset sign predictions
were committed to version control before any experiment was run, the rule scores
$8/8$; under an information-fair comparison, instance normalization loses to
plain global scaling on every backbone; and the Model Confidence Set retains
conditional normalization alone on every exogenous cell. LPS is thus a
near-zero-cost pre-training screen for the normalization layer of
energy-forecasting pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and contributions}
Renewable generation and electricity demand are forecasting problems in which
the target's \emph{level} is set by observable, physically grounded drivers.
Wind power is installed capacity times a weather-regime factor; photovoltaic
output follows a clear-sky geometry modulated by cloud cover; electricity load
responds to temperature through a degree-day relationship. In each case the
level to be predicted is largely \emph{exogenous} and observable in advance
through archived numerical weather prediction (NWP) forecasts available at the
forecast origin \citep{hong2016gefcom}.

Modern multi-step forecasters lead with instance normalization (RevIN and its
successors; \citealp{kim2022revin}), which removes each input window's mean and
variance before the backbone and restores them at the output. This is an
effective defense against train--test distribution shift, but the statistics it
restores are estimated \emph{endogenously}, from the target's own recent past.
On exogenously driven series it therefore discards level information the
covariates carry: the subtracted window mean is a stale summary precisely when a
weather ramp or heat wave is about to move the level, and the staleness
compounds with the forecast horizon. For climate applications this is the
common case, not a corner case---ramps and regime transitions are exactly the
events that drive reserve scheduling, curtailment, and renewable integration.

We ask when this default helps and when it hurts, and reduce the answer to a
quantity computable \emph{before} any model is trained. Our contributions are
(i)~a closed-form characterization of the boundary at which instance
normalization stops helping, in a linear--Gaussian model
(\S\ref{sec:boundary}); (ii)~a pre-training diagnostic---the Level
Predictability Score (LPS)---with an explicit decision rule
(\S\ref{sec:boundary}); and (iii)~an exogenous-versus-endogenous contrast of
\emph{normalization statistics}, evaluated in a pre-registered study whose
per-dataset sign predictions were committed to version control before any
experiment was run (\S\ref{sec:results}). We do not claim conditional
normalization as a new mechanism; the contribution is the boundary, the
diagnostic, and the pre-registered evidence that places each dataset on one
side of it.

\section{A closed-form boundary and a pre-training score}\label{sec:boundary}
Consider three level-handling strategies in a linear--Gaussian instance model:
global $z$-scoring (\rawm), instance normalization (\inm), and covariate-based
conditional normalization (\cnm), which estimates the target-time level from
exogenous covariates. Write $\lambda\in[0,1]$ for the exogenous predictability
of the window level, $h$ for the horizon, $V$ for total level variance, $\sx$
for within-horizon covariate-driven level change, $h\sigma_u^2$ for unexplained
drift, $\sdel$ for covariate-orthogonal train-to-test shift, and $\sest$ for the
first-stage error of \cnm. Then \inm{} pays the full within-horizon displacement
(it freezes the window statistic across the horizon) but is immune to level
variance and shift, whereas \cnm{} pays the unexplained level variance
$(1-\lambda)V$ but no within-horizon ramp.

\paragraph{Crossover (Prop.~2).} Estimated conditional normalization beats
instance normalization exactly when $\lambda > \lamstar$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lamstar}
\lamstar \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\sx + h\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_z^2/w - \sdel - \sest}{V}.
\end{equation}
$\lamstar$ increases in the covariate-orthogonal drift $\sdel$---delimiting,
not overturning, the distribution-shift rationale for RevIN.

\paragraph{Horizon (Prop.~3).} The gap is strictly increasing in the horizon,
$\partial_h\!\left[\mse_{\inm}-\mse_{\cnm}\right] = \sigma_u^2 + \partial_h \sx > 0$,
so $\lamstar(h)$ falls as $h$ grows: multi-step forecasting is the regime where
instance normalization is most exposed---confirmed on the exogenous group in
Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}. \emph{(Proofs, the stylized M1
construction, and the constrained-least-squares refinement are deferred to the
full version.)}

\paragraph{The pre-training diagnostic.} The coordinate $\lambda$ is
unobservable before training, so we estimate it out of sample. Partitioning the
training series into non-overlapping length-$w$ windows with means
$(\bar y_i,\bar x_i)$ and regressing the former on the latter under expanding
chronological cross-validation gives the Level Predictability Score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lps}
\lps \;=\; 1 \;-\;
\frac{\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_k}\bigl(\bar y_i-\hat g_k(\bar x_i)\bigr)^2}
     {\sum_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_k}\bigl(\bar y_i-\bar y^{\,\mathrm{tr}}_k\bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
with $\hat g_k$ (official instantiation: LightGBM, $w{=}96$) fit only on windows
preceding test block $\mathcal{T}_k$. The decision rule is one line:
\[
\lps \ge \tau \;\Rightarrow\; \text{conditional normalization}; \qquad
\text{otherwise instance normalization}; \qquad \tau = 0.3 .
\]
The threshold is anchored to theory---the stylized model places the crossover at
$\lamstar\approx 0.27$--$0.28$ at $h{=}24$, an upper bound on the true
crossing---so the rule is deliberately conservative toward instance
normalization.

Three results locate the boundary. \textbf{(a)~Pre-registration.} The official
$\lps$ value and the predicted sign of the RevIN$-$CondNorm test-MSE gap for
each dataset were committed to version control before any grid run; all eight
sign predictions were confirmed (Fig.~\ref{fig:lpsgap}). \textbf{(b)~Not just
more features.} Under an information-fair comparison that feeds every arm the
identical past and future covariates, RevIN$+$covariates is \emph{worse} than
\rawm$+$covariates on every backbone: a flexible model can learn the
covariate-to-level map from raw inputs, but none can recover a level its
normalizer has already removed---a function-class constraint, not an
information advantage. \textbf{(c)~Model Confidence Set.} In all eleven
exogenous (dataset, horizon) cells the Model Confidence Set retains conditional
normalization alone, whereas instance-normalization variants survive on the
standard benchmarks, where their distribution-shift rationale holds.

\section{Implications for energy operations and limitations}
For power-system operations the boundary is not academic: wind, solar, and load
are exactly the series whose levels move at ramps and regime transitions---the
events that drive reserve scheduling, curtailment, and balancing---where a
lookback-window mean is a stale summary and an archived weather forecast is
informative. Because $\lps$ needs only the covariates and a first-stage
regression already present in most operational pipelines, it is a
near-zero-cost pre-training screen: compute it once per series and let it decide
whether the normalization layer should adapt to the window (instance
normalization) or condition on the forecast drivers (conditional
normalization)---before committing GPU-hours to a model that a one-line
diagnostic would have flagged.

\paragraph{Pathway to climate impact.} Forecast quality on wind, solar, and
load feeds directly into grid decisions with an emissions footprint. The events
where the diagnostic matters most---ramps and regime transitions, where a
window mean is stale---are exactly the events that force operators to hold
fossil-fuelled spinning reserves, to curtail renewable output, and to dispatch
peaking plants for balancing. Sharper level forecasts at those moments tighten
reserve margins, reduce curtailment, and improve renewable integration---the
operational channel through which better forecasting lowers greenhouse-gas
emissions. The work also fits the workshop's ground-up theme: it begins from an
application-specific structure (a level that is exogenously determined and
observable in advance through weather forecasts) rather than a generic
architecture; the diagnostic is near-zero-cost---a first-stage regression
computable in seconds on a CPU, needing no forecasting model and no GPU
budget---so it drops into existing operational pipelines rather than demanding
new infrastructure; and both the code and a regional wind dataset we curated
(hourly generation with lead-matched archived weather forecasts) are released as
community resources.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Two caveats bound the claim. The rule is genuinely
stress-tested near its threshold in effectively a single case---Electricity is
the only dataset that lands close to $\tau$---and the wide $\tau\in[0.30,0.70]$
robustness plateau partly reflects the bimodal separation between the energy and
standard groups rather than fine calibration of the threshold. The full version
reports the diagnostic's permutation-test and confidence-interval companions and
leaves intermediate-predictability datasets as the key confirmatory task; the
pre-registered $8/8$ record and its commit-before-run ordering are unchanged.
